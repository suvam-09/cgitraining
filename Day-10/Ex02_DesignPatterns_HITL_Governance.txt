Answer:
1. Evaluator–Optimizer
Pattern: Generate → Evaluate → Refine

Real-life scenario:
An AI team designs an office-building façade. The Executor proposes a large glass façade. The Evaluator identifies excessive afternoon heat gain and sends feedback. The Executor revises the design using high-performance glazing and external shading. The Evaluator checks it again until the design meets the requirements.

Planner → Executor → Evaluator
              		↑          			│
              		└─ Refine ───┘
                   		 ↓
                 	Approve

HITL:
Replace the AI Evaluator with a human expert who reviews the proposed design and approves, rejects, or requests refinement.

Executor → HUMAN EVALUATOR → Approve / Refine

Override & Escalation:
If the issue exceeds the Evaluator's authority, escalate it to a human. 
Evaluator 	→ Minor issue → Refine
          		→ Major issue → Escalate → Human decision

Design Record:
Log the proposal, evaluation findings, refinement, final decision, human intervention, and rationale.

2. Prompt Chaining
Pattern: Sequential subtasks

Real-life scenario:
Design requirements → façade design → energy analysis → compliance check → final recommendation.

Requirements → Design → Energy → Compliance → Decision

HITL:
Insert a human review at a predefined step.

Override & Escalation:
A failed compliance check can stop the chain and escalate to a human.

Design Record:
Record the output and decision from each stage.

Key question: “What should happen next?”

3. Routing
Pattern: Classify → Dispatch

Real-life scenario:
An incoming building issue is classified as energy, structural, or compliance, then sent to the appropriate specialist agent.

              Issue
                ↓
              Router
          ↙      ↓      ↘
      Energy  Structural  Compliance

HITL:
If classification confidence is low, ask a human to classify it.

Override & Escalation:
A human can override the routing decision or escalate a high-risk issue.

Design Record:
Log the issue, classification, assigned agent, override, and resolution.

Key question: “Who should handle this?”

4. Orchestrator–Worker
Pattern: Dynamic task delegation

Real-life scenario:
The Orchestrator receives the goal of creating an energy-efficient façade and dynamically delegates tasks to energy, structural, and cost workers based on what it discovers.

              Goal
               ↓
         Orchestrator
        ↙      ↓      ↘
     Energy  Structural  Cost
        ↘      ↓      ↙
         Orchestrator
               ↓
            Decision

HITL:
A human intervenes when workers produce conflicting or high-risk results.

Override & Escalation:
The human can change the Orchestrator's priorities or require escalation.

Design Record:
Log delegated tasks, worker outputs, orchestration decisions, and human interventions.

Key question: “What work is needed next, and who should do it?”

5. Parallelization
Pattern: Fan-out → Fan-in

Real-life scenario:
Energy, cost, and compliance agents independently assess the same façade at the same time. Their results are then combined.

                  Façade
                    ↓
                 FAN-OUT
             ↙      ↓      ↘
          Energy   Cost   Compliance
             ↘      ↓      ↙
                 FAN-IN
                    ↓
              Combined result

HITL:
A human reviews the combined result when expert judgement is required.

Override & Escalation:
A critical failure, such as a compliance failure, can trigger escalation rather than automatic approval.

Design Record:
Log each parallel assessment, the combined result, conflicts, and final decision.

Key question: “What can happen at the same time?”
